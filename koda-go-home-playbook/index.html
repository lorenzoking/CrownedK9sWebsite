Your #1 priority

Potty training & daily rhythm

Here’s the truth: If you protect Koda’s potty rhythm, everything else becomes 10× easier.

The four potty triggers

Koda will need to go outside after these four events:

  1. After eating — 5–15 minutes after meals
  2. After drinking — plan for potty about 20 minutes after water
  3. After waking up — nap ends, potty starts
  4. After playing — when play slows down, go out immediately

Potty triggers visual reminder

Morning routine

Bathroom → Train (5–10 min) → Eat → Bathroom → Nap/alone time → Bathroom → Play → Bathroom

The rules

  • Take Koda out first thing when you see him in the morning.
  • Use “Hurry Up” every time; reward immediately when he goes.
  • After he drinks water, expect him to need to pee around 20 minutes later.
  • If he falls asleep, wake him up and take him out before he wakes up on his own.
  • If he doesn’t go: crate ~2 minutes → straight back outside. Repeat until he goes.
  • Limit water after 8:30 PM. Final potty right before bed.
  • At night (if needed): alarm every 4–5 hours for boring potty breaks if he truly needs one. Ice if thirsty, not a full water bowl.
Why this matters: Potty training is structure. Protect his rhythm and everything else falls into place.

Know your pup

Who Koda is & what to watch for

Koda’s personality

  • Very loyal to his person — he bonds deeply with the person who gives him clarity, structure, and consistency.
  • Selective with affection — he does not care for too much random human affection. Let him choose connection instead of forcing attention from strangers.
  • A little attitude / slight stubbornness — when you ask for a command he knows, be patient and make sure he follows through. Do not teach him that he can hear a known command and blow it off.
  • Neutral around people and dogs — he is less phased by other humans and dogs, which is an amazing trait for service-animal potential.
  • Very playful — he loves toys and does great when toys are used as a training reward.

Good things to look for

  • Reward offered calm: Sit, Down, eye contact, and settling without being asked.
  • Use toy rewards in training. A tug, fetch, or toy chase can be more meaningful than food in the right moment.
  • Keep name response valuable. When he hears “Koda”, it should mean “look at my person.”
  • Celebrate neutrality. If people or dogs are around and he stays focused or unbothered, pay that choice.

When he gets tired (important)

Koda gets zoomies when he is tired. That is usually not disobedience; it is a puppy needing a reset.

How to handle it:

  1. Calm, clear “No.” or "Hey!"
  2. Redirect to something appropriate: toy, chew, obedience command, crate nap, or short Place rep.
  3. Use the leash to prevent rehearsing bad choices, especially grabbing things off the ground.
  4. “Yes” and reward when he gives you attention instead.
  5. Use the zoomies as information: potty check if needed, then crate nap or quiet reset.

Pro tip: Tired puppies = trouble puppies. When behavior gets silly, think nap before more training.

How Koda learns: core concepts

These ideas shaped his training. They keep progress simple instead of overwhelming.

The staircase

Training is step by step. Start with heavy guidance (treats, leash, hands); fade help over time. If he struggles, take a step back. Don’t rush to the “top step.”

The cliffhanger

Sessions stay 1–2 minutes. End on success while he’s still engaged. Leave him wanting more.

The scoreboard (3 Ds)

A rep only “scores” if you release him. Held Sit 4 sec + “Free” = counts. Pushed to 10 sec and he broke himself = doesn’t count. Reset easier and win smaller.

Five reward types

Rotate: food, toys, praise, freedom, access (“Free,” sniff, run, explore, go to the yard). Mix rewards; don’t accidentally pay unwanted behavior with attention.

Luring: hands before words

Guide movement with food; mark “Yes” at the right instant; release with “Free.” Name the behavior after he understands the movement.

Say commands once

Say the command one time. If he does not respond, use silence, patience, body position, leash guidance, and hand motions to help him finish. If you can get your pup to do something without saying anything, then you should never have to say a command more than once.

Communication

Yes · Good · Free

Koda is trained on a simple, consistent system. These three words are the foundation.

Yes

Marks the exact moment he’s right: the bridge to the reward (replaces a clicker). Say it immediately, then pay him.

Good

Tells him to keep doing what he’s doing (duration on Sit, Place, etc.) without releasing yet.

Free

His release word: freedom after a command. He should not self-release from Sit, Down, Inside, Wait, or Place until “Free.” Use “Free” through the day so puppy life stays normal.

Your homework: Use Yes, Good, and Free all day. Always Yes before food/rewards. Always release with Free.

Maintenance & transfer

Commands Koda knows (part 1)

He understands these behaviors. Now he needs to learn that you speak this language too. Say each command once, then help him follow through with silence, hand motions, patience, and clear guidance.

Koda (name)

Means

Look at me.

How we taught it

Food near your face → sounds → the instant he meets your eyes, Yes → reward. Add “Koda” right before the look as it becomes consistent.

Your practice

Short games, several times a day. His name = eyes on you, not “I heard a noise.”

Sit

Means

Polite default for greetings, food, waiting.

Your practice

Before meals, doorways, greetings. Yes when his bottom hits the floor. Reward calm choices before excitement takes over.

Down

Means

Settle and relax.

Your practice

Capture downs throughout the day. If he offers a Down on his own, pay it. Calm choices should feel profitable.

Place

Means

Go to his spot and stay until released.

Your practice

Short reps; build duration with the scoreboard. Release at 5s, then 8s, then 10s. End on a win.

Come (recall)

Means

Run to me.

How it started

A game: “Come” only when he was already moving toward you for a stacked success rate.

Now

Use “Come” when you’re confident he’ll make it. If not sure, get attention first (name, movement), then call. Build proof in low distraction first.

One-command rule: Do not repeat commands he knows. Repeating teaches him the first word does not matter. Say it once, then calmly help him complete the behavior.

Maintenance & transfer

Commands & transfer notes (part 2)

Wait

Means

Pause. Don’t move through the threshold until Free.

Your practice

Meals, crate door, front door, gates. If he can’t wait at doorways, impulse control outside gets harder.

Inside (crate)

Means

Go in the crate on his own.

Your practice

Make the crate a choice: treats, toys, chews. Daily alone time in crate helps prevent separation anxiety.

Toy rewards

Means

Training can be paid with play, not just food.

Your practice

Use a toy as the reward after obedience reps: Sit → Yes → tug, Down → Yes → toy chase, Come → Yes → play. Keep the toy controlled so it rewards the behavior you asked for.

Commands still in progress

Koda does not know Touch or Leave It yet. Do not assume those words have meaning. Build them later as new skills with the same system: lure or guide, mark with Yes, reward, then name the behavior after he understands it.

Hurry Up

Means

Go potty now.

Your practice

Say it once as he starts; stay neutral. When he finishes, Yes and a big payoff.

No Biting

Means

Stop mouthing hands.

Your practice

Calm “No Biting,” redirect to a toy, reward for choosing the toy. Consistency wins. This phase passes.

Calm comes from clarity

Boundaries, focus, & household manners

Doorway manners

Koda is released through every doorway: inside, outside, gates, crate. He pauses until Free. This builds impulse control, safety, and respect for thresholds.

Golden rule: If he can’t control doorways, he won’t control distractions outside. Master this first.

Commands happen once

Say the word once, then use silence and guidance. If Koda hears “Sit, sit, sit,” he learns the first two did not matter. Your patience after the first command is what teaches him to listen the first time.

Boundaries = clarity = calm

Be consistent on furniture rules, food waits, and doorways. Dogs want expectations, not chaos.

Focus & engagement

Reward eye contact and calm choices. Choosing you unlocks rewards. Keep that bank account full.
